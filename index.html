Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or
obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it
has changed, where it concentrates, what it costs the
health system, and how it is treated.

Population Profile and Trends

Who is affected, and how the picture has changed
over a decade.

Who is affected?

The pyramid puts males on the left and females
on the right. Among children aged 2 to 17,
between about 17% and 32% of each age and
sex group are overweight or obese, compared with 73%
of adult men and 62% of adult women.

In adulthood 34.1% of men and 31.7% of women
are obese (dark bars), against 13.7% of boys and
8.1% of girls aged 16 to 17. Estimates for
young children come from small samples, so treat boy
and girl differences at the youngest ages with caution.

Is it getting worse?

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose
from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. Rates
dipped slightly between 2011–12 and 2014–15, but the shaded
confidence bands overlap, so that dip may not be
meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year. By
2017–18 the gap had widened to about three percentage
points (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Geographic Distribution

Where obesity is concentrated across Australia, who lives there,
and how men and women differ.

Where is obesity highest?

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity
in each Population Health Area. Darker areas have higher
rates; grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very
remote regions. The bars rank the 25 highest areas,
and clicking a bar highlights it on the map.

The highest rate is in Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.)
at 55.3%, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and
Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in
inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4%), Adelaide (14.8%)
and Melbourne CBD (15.8%).

Do men and women differ everywhere?

This map asks a different question from the one
above: how much higher is the male obesity rate
than the female rate? Each hexagon averages the areas
inside it, so crowded city areas stay readable. Males
have the higher rate in 1,121 of 1,134 areas
with estimates, and the typical gap is 1.9 percentage
points.

Gaps are widest, at up to 2.8 points, around
George Town in north-east Tasmania and Traralgon in Victoria,
and 2.6 points around Palmerston in the Northern Territory.
Around Ellenbrook, in Perth's north-east, the gap is only
1.1 points.

Where do the people live?

A high rate in a sparsely populated area affects
few people, while a moderate rate in a city
affects many. Here circle size shows the estimated number
of adults with obesity in each area. Large circles
cluster along the east and south-east coasts and in
south-west Western Australia.

New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland together account for
roughly 77% of the estimated 6.3 million adults with
obesity in areas with estimates.

Social Determinants of Obesity

Which social groups carry the most risk, and how
that has changed.

Is education a ladder?

Obesity rose at every education level between 2007–08 and
2017–18, and the gap between the highest and lowest
levels widened from 12.2 to 17.8 percentage points. Adults
who did not finish secondary school rose from 29.3%
to 38.3%, while bachelor degree holders rose only from
17.1% to 20.5%.

The steepest climb was among adults with a diploma
(+13.7 points), who moved from near the bottom to
close to the middle.

Which jobs carry the most risk?

Ranking the eight occupation groups by obesity rate shows
two fixed points: machinery operators and drivers rank first
in every survey (36.3% in 2017–18) and professionals rank
last in every survey (22.6%).

The middle of the table reshuffles. Community and personal
service workers jumped to 2nd in 2011–12 before falling
to 7th, and managers moved from 4th to 7th
and back up to 3rd. Rankings can move on
small changes in rate, so only the two ends
are reliable.

Do household circumstances matter?

Differences are smaller here, but obesity still rose in
every group. In 2017–18 the spread is 4.6 points
across employment groups (employed 28.6% vs not in the
labour force 33.2%) and 5.6 points across household types,
where one-parent families are highest (33.7%).

Marital status and tenure differ by only about 2
to 3 points: married adults 29.1% vs not married
31.6%, and renters 31.0% vs owners without a mortgage
28.8%.

Which factors divide obesity most?

For each social factor, this chart connects the group
with the lowest obesity rate (teal) to the group
with the highest (wine) in 2017–18. Six factors show
gaps of more than 8 percentage points.

Education is the widest at 17.8 points: 38.3% of
adults who did not finish secondary school were obese
compared with 20.5% of those with a bachelor degree
or higher. Language spoken at home follows (15.3 points),
then occupation (13.7), country of birth (10.0), household income
(8.9) and remoteness (8.6).

Health System Impact

What obesity means for hospitals and for health spending.

Why do people with obesity go to hospital?

These are the 20 most common principal diagnoses among
hospital stays where obesity was also recorded. Osteoarthritis of
the knee is the most common, with 21,258 stays,
followed by cataract (16,365) and caesarean delivery (8,964).

Bar colour shows how often obesity is recorded among
all stays for that diagnosis. The darkest bars are
gallstones with cholecystitis (14.0%), cellulitis of the lower limb
(13.4%), sleep apnoea (12.6%) and knee osteoarthritis (12.2%), while
cataract (5.3%) and cancer drug therapy (1.0%) are much
lower.

What does it cost?

Health spending linked to overweight and obesity in 2023–24
totals about $10.8 billion across the age groups shown.
Only about $0.8 billion is spent directly on obesity
treatment; the rest goes to treating conditions caused by
excess weight.

Spending peaks at ages 65 to 69 for both
sexes (about $857 million for males and $670 million
for females). Males account for about $5.7 billion and
females $5.1 billion.

About this dashboard

  • Audience and purpose: a general audience who want to
    understand obesity in Australia: where it is concentrated and
    what it costs, who is affected and how that
    has changed, and which social factors divide it most.
  • One idiom per question: population pyramid and line with
    confidence bands (who and when); choropleth with ranked bars,
    hexagon map and proportional symbol map (where); slope chart,
    bump chart, heatmap and connected dot plot (social determinants);
    bar chart and butterfly chart (health system); and a
    bivariate map with a linked scatterplot (overlap with a
    social measure). Charts sit side by side where they
    answer related questions.
  • One colour theme, colour-blind safe: the same colours mean
    the same thing everywhere. Males are blue and females
    vermillion (Okabe–Ito); wine marks the highest-obesity group and teal
    the lowest; ordered weight categories use a Paul Tol
    blue–sand–wine set that differs in lightness as well as
    hue; and sequential data uses viridis, where darker always
    means a higher rate. Colour is never the only
    cue: charts also use direct labels, marker shapes, line
    styles and printed values.
  • Reading the charts: measured axes start at zero, grid
    lines mark every scale, percentages carry a % sign,
    and units (percentage points, $ million, stays, adults) are
    named in labels and tooltips. Where the sources give
    95% confidence intervals they are shown so small differences
    are not over-read.
  • Data: AIHW overweight and obesity tables (S5, S15, S21,
    S29), hospital separations with U78.1, and PHIDU modelled estimates
    for Population Health Areas (2022). Boundaries: ABS Population Health
    Areas 2021, simplified for web use.
