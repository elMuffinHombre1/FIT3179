Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it has changed, where it concentrates, what it costs the health system, and how it is treated.

Who is affected? One in three adults, one in four children

Imagine 100 Australian adults. About 34 are overweight but not obese and about 33 are obese, so roughly two in three are above a healthy weight. Among children aged 2 to 17, around 27% are overweight or obese, including 8.5% who are obese.

Each square is one person, which makes the shares easy to compare between adults and children without reading a scale.

Sources: AIHW Table S15 (children, 2022–24) and the adult BMI table (2022). For adults, "not overweight" includes the 1.5% who are underweight. Squares are rounded to whole people.

Is it getting worse? A decade of rising obesity

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. The trend was not a straight line: rates dipped slightly between 2011–12 and 2014–15, although the shaded confidence bands overlap, so that dip may not be meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year, and by 2017–18 the gap had widened to about three percentage points (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Source: AIHW Table S5 (age-standardised, adults 18–64). The series ends in 2017–18 because that is the latest year in the supplied table.

Why does it differ? Obesity across social groups

This heatmap shows every social group at all four survey years. Reading across a row shows change over time, and reading down a block compares groups. Almost every row gets darker towards the right, so obesity rose across nearly all of society. The only group to fall was adults who speak a language other than English at home (18.7% to 16.7%).

The darkest cells sit with adults who did not finish secondary school (38.3% in 2017–18), machinery operators and drivers (36.3%), and people in inner regional areas (36.3%). The lightest sit with adults who speak a language other than English at home (16.7%), bachelor degree holders (20.5%) and professionals (22.6%).

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64. Hover over a cell for the confidence interval.

How big are the gaps? The widest social divides

Within each social factor, this chart connects the group with the lowest obesity rate to the group with the highest in 2017–18. Longer lines mean larger inequality.

Education has the widest gap at 17.8 percentage points, followed by language spoken at home (15.3) and occupation (13.7). Household income (8.9) and remoteness (8.6) also show large gaps, while household tenure (2.2) and marital status (2.5) show small ones. Groups labelled "Other" were left out because they are not a meaningful comparison.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised obesity rate, adults 18–64, 2017–18.

Living with disability: obesity and core activity limitations

Adults with a core activity limitation are more likely to live with obesity: 43.8% compared with 28.9% of those without. Severe obesity is about twice as common (21.4% vs 10.5%).

The risk is not highest at the most severe end. Obesity peaks among people with a moderate limitation (51.8%). The profound group has a wide confidence interval because of small sample sizes, so treat that value with caution.

Source: AIHW Table S21, persons 18+, 2022. Lollipops show the estimate; thin dark bars show the 95% confidence interval.

Where does it concentrate? Obesity across Australia

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity (age-standardised, per 100 people) in each Population Health Area. Darker areas have higher rates. Grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very remote regions.

The highest rate is in Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.) at 55.3 per 100, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4), Adelaide (14.8) and Melbourne CBD (15.8).

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates of adults 18+ who were obese, 2022. These are modelled estimates, not direct counts.

Is the state the story? Variation inside each state

State averages hide a lot. Each dot below is one Population Health Area, with a bar marking the state's median. Across the 1,134 areas with estimates the rate runs from 13.4 to 55.3 per 100, a spread of more than 40 points.

Medians range only from 28.9 in the ACT to 39.9 in Tasmania, a spread of about 11 points, while within NSW, Victoria and Queensland the areas span roughly 30 to 40 points. Where you live inside a state matters as much as which state you live in.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates, 2022. Dots are jittered sideways only to avoid overlap; horizontal position carries no meaning.

State by state: a fair-sized view of every jurisdiction

On a normal map, Tasmania and the ACT almost disappear while Western Australia dominates. Here every state and territory is the same size. Tasmania has the highest approximate obesity rate (37.1%), followed by the Northern Territory and Queensland (about 35%). The ACT (29.3%) and Victoria (29.9%) are lowest.

Source: calculated from PHIDU area estimates. State rates are approximate: each area's rate is weighted by a proxy for its adult population, and areas without an estimate are excluded (this matters most for the Northern Territory, where 9 of 14 areas have estimates).

Where do the people live? Numbers, not just rates

A high rate in a sparsely populated area affects few people, while a moderate rate in a city affects many. Here circle size shows the estimated number of adults with obesity in each area. Large circles cluster along the east and south-east coasts and in south-west Western Australia.

New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland together account for roughly 77% of the estimated 6.3 million adults with obesity in areas with estimates, which is why health services in these states carry the largest absolute load.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates, 2022. Circles are placed at an interior point of each area, not its exact population centre.

What puts people in hospital? Comorbidities and hospital stays

These are the 20 most common principal diagnoses among hospital stays where obesity was also recorded. The left side ranks them by number of stays; the right ranks them by how often obesity is recorded among all stays for that diagnosis. Lines that climb steeply show conditions with a strong obesity link that are not necessarily common overall.

Gallstones with cholecystitis is only 16th by volume but ranks 1st by share (14.0%). Cellulitis of the lower limb jumps from 9th to 2nd, and sleep apnoea and heavy menstrual bleeding also climb. By contrast, cataract surgery (5.3%) and caesarean delivery (8.1%) are common but are comparatively less often coded with obesity.

Source: hospital separations with a supplementary U78.1 (obesity) code. This shows how often obesity is recorded, not that it caused the admission.

What does it cost? Spending by age and sex

Health spending linked to overweight and obesity in 2023–24 totals about $10.8 billion across the age groups shown. Only about $0.8 billion is spent directly on obesity treatment and management; the rest goes to treating conditions caused by excess weight.

Spending is concentrated in later life, peaking at ages 65 to 69 for both sexes (about $857 million for males and $670 million for females). Males account for about $5.7 billion and females $5.1 billion. Direct treatment is a larger share of female spending in younger and middle adulthood.

Source: AIHW Table S29, 2023–24. Values converted from dollars to millions.

About this dashboard

  • Audience and purpose: a general audience who want to understand obesity in Australia, moving from who is affected, to why, to where, to what it costs.
  • One idiom per question: waffle (shares of 100 people), line with confidence bands (change over time), heatmap (many groups across four years), connected dot plot (gaps between groups), lollipop (estimates with uncertainty), choropleth with ranked bars, jitter plot (every area, not just averages), tile grid map (equal-sized states), proportional symbol map (counts, not rates), slope chart (rank changes) and butterfly chart (age and sex).
  • Colour-blind safe colour: males and females use the blue/vermillion pair from the Okabe–Ito palette; ordered categories use a Paul Tol blue–sand–wine set that differs in lightness as well as hue; sequential data uses the viridis scale, where darker always means a higher rate. Colour is never the only cue: charts also use direct labels, different marker shapes and line styles, and printed values.
  • Uncertainty: where the sources give 95% confidence intervals they are shown (bands, bars or tooltips) so small differences are not over-read.
  • Data: AIHW overweight and obesity tables (S5, S15, S21, S29), hospital separations with U78.1, and PHIDU modelled estimates for Population Health Areas (2022). Boundaries: ABS Population Health Areas 2021, simplified for web use.
