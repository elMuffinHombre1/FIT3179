Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it has changed, where it concentrates, what it costs the health system, and how it is treated.

Geography and Health System Burden

Where obesity is concentrated across Australia, and what it costs the health system.

Where is obesity highest?

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity (age-standardised, per 100 people) in each Population Health Area. Darker areas have higher rates; grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very remote regions.

The highest rate is in Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.) at 55.3 per 100, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4), Adelaide (14.8) and Melbourne CBD (15.8).

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates of adults 18+ who were obese, 2022. These are modelled estimates, not direct counts.

How do the states compare?

On a normal map, Tasmania and the ACT almost disappear while Western Australia dominates. Here every state and territory is the same size. Tasmania has the highest approximate obesity rate (37.1%), followed by the Northern Territory and Queensland (about 35%). The ACT (29.3%) and Victoria (29.9%) are lowest.

Source: calculated from PHIDU area estimates. State rates are approximate: each area's rate is weighted by a proxy for its adult population, and areas without an estimate are excluded (this matters most for the Northern Territory, where 9 of 14 areas have estimates).

Where do the people live?

A high rate in a sparsely populated area affects few people, while a moderate rate in a city affects many. Here circle size shows the estimated number of adults with obesity in each area. Large circles cluster along the east and south-east coasts and in south-west Western Australia.

New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland together account for roughly 77% of the estimated 6.3 million adults with obesity in areas with estimates.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates, 2022. Circles are placed at an interior point of each area, not its exact population centre.

Which conditions are most linked to obesity in hospital?

These are the 20 most common principal diagnoses among hospital stays where obesity was also recorded. The left side ranks them by number of stays; the right ranks them by how often obesity is recorded among all stays for that diagnosis. Lines that climb steeply show conditions with a strong obesity link that are not necessarily common overall.

Gallstones with cholecystitis is only 16th by volume but ranks 1st by share (14.0%). Cellulitis of the lower limb jumps from 9th to 2nd, and sleep apnoea and heavy menstrual bleeding also climb. By contrast, cataract surgery (5.3%) and caesarean delivery (8.1%) are common but comparatively less often coded with obesity.

Source: hospital separations with a supplementary U78.1 (obesity) code. This shows how often obesity is recorded, not that it caused the admission.

What does it cost?

Health spending linked to overweight and obesity in 2023–24 totals about $10.8 billion across the age groups shown. Only about $0.8 billion is spent directly on obesity treatment and management; the rest goes to treating conditions caused by excess weight.

Spending is concentrated in later life, peaking at ages 65 to 69 for both sexes (about $857 million for males and $670 million for females). Males account for about $5.7 billion and females $5.1 billion. Direct treatment is a larger share of female spending in younger and middle adulthood.

Source: AIHW Table S29, 2023–24. Values converted from dollars to millions.

Population Profile and Trends

Who is affected, and how the picture has changed over a decade.

Who is affected? From childhood to adulthood

The pyramid places males on the left and females on the right. Among children aged 2 to 17, between about 17% and 32% of each age and sex group are overweight or obese, compared with 73% of adult men and 62% of adult women. In adulthood 34.1% of men and 31.7% of women are obese (dark bars), against 13.7% of boys and 8.1% of girls aged 16 to 17.

Estimates for young children come from small samples with wide confidence intervals, so treat differences between boys and girls at the youngest ages with caution. Children and adults are classified differently, so read the shape of the pyramid rather than comparing exact values.

Sources: AIHW Table S15 (children, 2022–24) and the adult BMI table (2022). Obese for adults combines BMI classes I to III.

Is it getting worse?

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. The trend was not a straight line: rates dipped slightly between 2011–12 and 2014–15, although the shaded confidence bands overlap, so that dip may not be meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year, and by 2017–18 the gap had widened to about three percentage points (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Source: AIHW Table S5 (age-standardised, adults 18–64). The series ends in 2017–18 because that is the latest year in the supplied table.

Social Determinants: The Major Divides

The social factors that separate high and low obesity the most.

Which factors divide obesity most?

For each social factor, this chart connects the group with the lowest obesity rate (teal) to the group with the highest (wine) in 2017–18. Six factors show gaps of more than 8 points.

Education is the widest at 17.8 points: 38.3% of adults who did not finish secondary school were obese compared with 20.5% of those with a bachelor degree or higher. Language spoken at home follows (15.3 points), then occupation (13.7), country of birth (10.0), household income (8.9) and remoteness (8.6).

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised obesity rate, adults 18–64, 2017–18. Groups labelled "Other" were left out.

Is education a ladder?

Obesity rose at every education level between 2007–08 and 2017–18, and the gap between the highest and lowest levels widened from 12.2 to 17.8 points. Adults who did not finish secondary school rose from 29.3% to 38.3%, while bachelor degree holders rose only from 17.1% to 20.5%.

The steepest climb was among adults with a diploma or advanced diploma (+13.7 points), who moved from near the bottom to close to the middle of the pack.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64.

Which jobs carry the most risk?

Ranking the eight occupation groups by obesity rate shows two fixed points: machinery operators and drivers rank first in every survey (36.3% in 2017–18) and professionals rank last in every survey (22.6%).

The middle of the table reshuffles. Community and personal service workers jumped to 2nd in 2011–12 before falling to 7th, managers moved from 4th to 7th and back up to 3rd, and technicians and trades workers slipped from 3rd to 5th. Rankings can move on small changes in rate, so only the two ends are consistently reliable.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64. Rank 1 = highest obesity rate.

Social Determinants: Smaller Differences

Factors where the gaps between groups are narrower.

Do household circumstances matter?

Differences are smaller here, but obesity still rose in every group. In 2017–18 the spread is 4.6 points across employment groups (employed 28.6% vs not in the labour force 33.2%) and 5.6 points across household types, where one-parent families are highest (33.7%).

Marital status and tenure differ by only about 2 to 3 points: married adults 29.1% vs not married 31.6%, and renters 31.0% vs owners without a mortgage 28.8%.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64. Hover over a cell for the confidence interval.

About this dashboard

  • Audience and purpose: a general audience who want to understand obesity in Australia: where it is concentrated and what it costs, who is affected and how that has changed, and which social factors divide it most.
  • One idiom per question: choropleth with ranked bars, tile grid map and proportional symbol map (place); slope charts (rank and change); butterfly chart and population pyramid (age and sex); line with confidence bands (trend); connected dot plot (gaps); bump chart (rank over time); heatmap (many groups across years); and a bivariate map with a linked scatterplot (overlap with a social measure).
  • One colour theme, colour-blind safe: the same colours mean the same thing everywhere. Males are blue and females vermillion (Okabe–Ito); wine marks the highest-obesity group and teal the lowest; ordered weight categories use a Paul Tol blue–sand–wine set that differs in lightness as well as hue; and sequential data uses viridis, where darker always means a higher rate. Colour is never the only cue: charts also use direct labels, marker shapes, line styles and printed values.
  • Uncertainty: where the sources give 95% confidence intervals they are shown (bands, bars or tooltips) so small differences are not over-read.
  • Data: AIHW overweight and obesity tables (S5, S15, S21, S29), hospital separations with U78.1, and PHIDU modelled estimates for Population Health Areas (2022). Boundaries: ABS Population Health Areas 2021, simplified for web use.
