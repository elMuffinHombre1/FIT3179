Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or
obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it
has changed, where it concentrates, what it costs the
health system, and how it is treated.

Population Profile and Trends

Who is affected, and how the picture has changed
over a decade.

Who is affected?

The pyramid puts males on the left and females
on the right. Among children aged 2 to 17,
between about 17% and 32% of each age and
sex group are overweight or obese, compared with 73%
of adult men and 62% of adult women.

In adulthood 34.1% of men and 31.7% of women
are obese (dark bars), against 13.7% of boys and
8.1% of girls aged 16 to 17. Estimates for
young children come from small samples, so treat boy
and girl differences at the youngest ages with caution.

Sources: AIHW Table S15 (children, 2022–24) and the adult
BMI table (2022). Obese for adults combines BMI classes
I to III. Children and adults are classified differently,
so compare shapes, not exact values.

Is it getting worse?

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose
from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. Rates
dipped slightly between 2011–12 and 2014–15, but the shaded
confidence bands overlap, so that dip may not be
meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year. By
2017–18 the gap had widened to about three percentage
points (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Source: AIHW Table S5 (age-standardised, adults 18–64). The series
ends in 2017–18 because that is the latest year
in the supplied table.

Geographic Distribution

Where obesity is concentrated across Australia, who lives there,
and how men and women differ.

Where is obesity highest?

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity
in each Population Health Area. Darker areas have higher
rates; grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very
remote regions. The bars rank the 25 highest areas,
and clicking a bar highlights it on the map.

The highest rate is in Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.)
at 55.3%, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and
Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in
inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4%), Adelaide (14.8%)
and Melbourne CBD (15.8%).

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates of adults 18+ who were
obese, 2022. These are modelled estimates, not direct counts.

Do men and women differ everywhere?

This map asks a different question from the one
above: how much higher is the male obesity rate
than the female rate? Each hexagon averages the areas
inside it, so crowded city areas stay readable. Males
have the higher rate in 1,121 of 1,134 areas
with estimates, and the typical gap is 1.9 percentage
points.

Gaps are widest, at up to 2.8 points, around
George Town in north-east Tasmania and Traralgon in Victoria,
and 2.6 points around Palmerston in the Northern Territory.
Around Ellenbrook, in Perth's north-east, the gap is only
1.1 points.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates by sex, adults 18+, 2022.
Hexagons are weighted by the estimated number of adults
with obesity in each area.

Where do the people live?

A high rate in a sparsely populated area affects
few people, while a moderate rate in a city
affects many. Here circle size shows the estimated number
of adults with obesity in each area. Large circles
cluster along the east and south-east coasts and in
south-west Western Australia.

New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland together account for
roughly 77% of the estimated 6.3 million adults with
obesity in areas with estimates.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates, 2022. Circles are placed at
an interior point of each area, not its exact
population centre.

Social Determinants of Obesity

Which social groups carry the most risk, and how
that has changed.

Is education a ladder?

Obesity rose at every education level between 2007–08 and
2017–18, and the gap between the highest and lowest
levels widened from 12.2 to 17.8 percentage points. Adults
who did not finish secondary school rose from 29.3%
to 38.3%, while bachelor degree holders rose only from
17.1% to 20.5%.

The steepest climb was among adults with a diploma
(+13.7 points), who moved from near the bottom to
close to the middle.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults
