Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it has changed, where it concentrates, which social factors divide it, and what it costs the health system.

Section 1

Population Profile and Trends

Who is affected, and how the picture has changed over a decade.

Chart 1

Who is affected?

The pyramid puts males in blue and females in orange. Among children aged 2 to 17, between about 17% and 32% of each age and sex group are overweight or obese, compared with 73% of adult men and 62% of adult women.

In adulthood 34.1% of men and 31.7% of women are obese (dark bars), against 13.7% of boys and 8.1% of girls aged 16 to 17. Estimates for young children come from small samples, so treat boy and girl differences at the youngest ages with caution.

Sources: AIHW Table S15 (children, 2022–24) and the adult BMI table (2022). Obese for adults combines BMI classes I to III. Children and adults are classified differently, so compare shapes, not exact values.

Chart 2

Is it getting worse?

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. Rates dipped slightly between 2011–12 and 2014–15, but the shaded confidence bands overlap, so that dip may not be meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year. By 2017–18 the gap had widened to about three percentage points (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Source: AIHW Table S5 (age-standardised, adults 18–64). The series ends in 2017–18 because that is the latest year in the supplied table.

Section 2

Geographic Distribution

Where obesity is concentrated across Australia, who lives there, and how men and women differ.

Chart 3

Where is obesity highest?

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity in each Population Health Area. Darker areas have higher rates; grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very remote regions. The bars rank the 25 highest areas, and clicking a bar highlights it on the map.

The highest rate is in Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.) at 55.3%, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4%), Adelaide (14.8%) and Melbourne CBD (15.8%).

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates of adults 18+ who were obese, 2022. These are modelled estimates, not direct counts.

Chart 4

Do men and women differ everywhere?

Each hexagon shows how much higher male obesity is than female. Males are higher in 1,121 of 1,134 areas, by 1.9 points typically.

The gap peaks at 2.8 points near George Town (Tas.) and Traralgon (Vic.).

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates by sex, adults 18+, 2022.

Chart 5

Where do the people live?

Circle size shows the number of adults with obesity, so cities stand out. Large circles cluster on the east and south-east coasts.

NSW, Victoria and Queensland hold about 77% of the 6.3 million adults.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates, 2022. Circles sit at an interior point of each area.

Section 3

Social Determinants of Obesity

Which social groups carry the most risk, and how that has changed.

Chart 6

Is education a ladder?

Obesity rose at every education level between 2007–08 and 2017–18, and the gap between the highest and lowest levels widened from 12.2 to 17.8 percentage points. Adults who did not finish secondary school rose from 29.3% to 38.3%, while bachelor degree holders rose only from 17.1% to 20.5%.

The steepest climb was among adults with a diploma (+13.7 points), who moved from near the bottom to close to the middle.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64.

Chart 7

Which jobs carry the most risk?

Ranking the eight occupation groups by obesity rate shows two fixed points: machinery operators and drivers rank first in every survey (36.3% in 2017–18) and professionals rank last in every survey (22.6%).

The middle of the table reshuffles. Community and personal service workers jumped to 2nd in 2011–12 before falling to 7th, and managers moved from 4th to 7th and back up to 3rd. Rankings can move on small changes in rate, so only the two ends are reliable.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64. Rank 1 = highest obesity rate.

Chart 8

Do household circumstances matter?

Differences are smaller here, but obesity still rose in every group. In 2017–18 the spread is 4.6 points across employment groups (employed 28.6% vs not in the labour force 33.2%) and 5.6 points across household types, where one-parent families are highest (33.7%).

Marital status and tenure differ by only about 2 to 3 points: married adults 29.1% vs not married 31.6%, and renters 31.0% vs owners without a mortgage 28.8%.

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages, adults 18–64. Hover over a cell for the confidence interval.

Chart 9

Which factors divide obesity most?

For each social factor, this chart connects the group with the lowest obesity rate (teal) to the group with the highest (wine) in 2017–18. Six factors show gaps of more than 8 percentage points.

Education is the widest at 17.8 points: 38.3% of adults who did not finish secondary school were obese compared with 20.5% of those with a bachelor degree or higher. Language spoken at home follows (15.3 points), then occupation (13.7), country of birth (10.0), household income (8.9) and remoteness (8.6).

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised obesity rate, adults 18–64, 2017–18. Groups labelled "Other" were left out.

Section 4

Health System Impact

What obesity means for hospitals and for health spending.

Chart 10

Why do people with obesity go to hospital?

These are the 20 most common principal diagnoses among hospital stays where obesity was also recorded. Osteoarthritis of the knee is the most common, with 21,258 stays, followed by cataract (16,365) and caesarean delivery (8,964).

Bar colour shows how often obesity is recorded among all stays for that diagnosis. The darkest bars are gallstones with cholecystitis (14.0%), cellulitis of the lower limb (13.4%), sleep apnoea (12.6%) and knee osteoarthritis (12.2%), while cataract (5.3%) and cancer drug therapy (1.0%) are much lower.

Source: hospital separations with a supplementary U78.1 (obesity) code. This shows how often obesity is recorded, not that it caused the admission.

Chart 11

What does it cost?

Health spending linked to overweight and obesity in 2023–24 totals about $10.8 billion across the age groups shown. Only about $0.8 billion is spent directly on obesity treatment; the rest goes to treating conditions caused by excess weight.

Spending peaks at ages 65 to 69 for both sexes (about $857 million for males and $670 million for females). Males account for about $5.7 billion and females $5.1 billion.

Source: AIHW Table S29, 2023–24. Values converted from dollars to millions.
