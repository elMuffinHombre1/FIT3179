Obesity in Australia

Roughly two in three Australian adults are overweight or obese. This dashboard explains who is affected, how it has changed, where it concentrates, what it costs the health system, and how it is treated.

Who is affected? The BMI profile of Australian adults

About two in three Australian adults are above a healthy weight. Across males and females combined, 34.3% are overweight but not obese and 32.8% are obese (classes I to III together). The pyramid splits the adult population by body mass index category so the two sexes can be compared directly.

Males are more likely than females to be overweight (38.4% vs 30.2%) and to be in obesity class I (21.9% vs 17.7%). At the severe end the pattern reverses: 5.4% of females are in obesity class III (BMI 40+) compared with 3.9% of males.

Source: AIHW, adult BMI by sex (Gender-Rate table). Percentages with 95% confidence intervals are shown in the tooltips.

Is it getting worse? Obesity over time

Among adults aged 18 to 64, age-standardised obesity rose from 23.7% in 2007–08 to 29.6% in 2017–18. The shaded bands are 95% confidence intervals, so overlapping bands should be read as differences that may not be statistically meaningful.

Males sit above females in every survey year, and the gap widened to roughly three percentage points by 2017–18 (31.3% vs 28.0%).

Source: AIHW Table S5 (age-standardised, adults 18–64). The series ends at 2017–18 because that is the latest year in the supplied table.

Why does it differ? Social determinants

Obesity is not spread evenly across society. Each row compares 2007–08 (grey) with 2017–18 (red) for adults aged 18 to 64, grouped by a social factor. Nearly every group moved to the right, meaning obesity increased across the board.

The gaps are large. In 2017–18, 38.3% of adults who did not complete secondary school were obese compared with 20.5% of those with a bachelor degree or higher. Adults in the lowest income quintile (34.3%) were more affected than those in the highest (25.4%), and people in inner regional areas (36.3%) more than those in major cities (27.7%). The one group that fell was adults who speak a language other than English at home (16.7%).

Source: AIHW Table S5, age-standardised percentages. Occupation categories for unemployed and not in the labour force are shown under Employment only.

Where does it concentrate? Obesity across Australia

The map shows the modelled rate of adult obesity (age-standardised rate per 100 people) in each of Australia's Population Health Areas. Darker areas have higher rates. Light grey areas have no published estimate, mostly very remote regions.

The ranked bar chart lists the 25 areas with the highest rates. The highest is Bridgewater – Gagebrook (Tas.) at 55.3 per 100, followed by New Chum/Redbank Plains (Qld) and Tamworth – West (NSW). The lowest rates are in inner-city areas such as Haymarket/Millers Point/Sydney (13.4), Adelaide (14.8) and Melbourne CBD (15.8). The median across areas with data is 33.2.

Source: PHIDU, modelled estimates of adults 18+ who were obese, 2022. These are modelled estimates, not direct counts. Click a bar to highlight it.

Living with disability: obesity and core limitations

People with core activity limitations are more likely to live with obesity. Across males and females combined, 43.8% of adults with a core limitation are obese compared with 28.9% of those without. Severe obesity is more than twice as common (21.4% vs 10.5%).

The chart breaks this down by the severity of the limitation and by sex. Error bars are 95% confidence intervals; the wide intervals for the profound category reflect small sample sizes.

Source: AIHW Table S21, adults 18+, 2022.

Comorbidities: what do hospital patients with obesity come in for?

This chart counts hospital stays where obesity (code U78.1) was recorded alongside another main diagnosis. Out of 540,913 such stays, the most common principal diagnosis was osteoarthritis of the knee (21,258 stays, 3.9%), followed by cataract, caesarean delivery, hip osteoarthritis and cancer drug therapy sessions.

Many of the conditions linked to excess weight, such as sleep apnoea, heart failure, atrial fibrillation and gallbladder disease, appear in the list.

Source: Hospital separations with a supplementary U78.1 code. This shows how often obesity is recorded, not that obesity caused the admission.

Hospitalisations: how often is obesity recorded?

The previous chart ranks diagnoses by volume. This one asks a different question: for each diagnosis, what share of all hospital stays also had obesity recorded? The share is highest for gallbladder disease with cholecystitis (14.0%), cellulitis of the lower limb (13.4%), obstructive sleep apnoea (12.6%) and knee osteoarthritis (12.2%).

Source: calculated from the comorbidity table as separations with U78.1 divided by total separations for that diagnosis.

What does it cost? Health expenditure by age and sex

Health spending linked to overweight and obesity in 2023–24 totals about $10.8 billion across the age groups shown. Only about $0.8 billion of this is spent directly on treating and managing obesity. The remaining $10.1 billion goes to treating conditions caused by excess weight.

Spending climbs steeply with age, peaking at ages 65 to 69 for both sexes. Males account for about $5.7 billion and females about $5.1 billion. Direct obesity treatment spending is higher for females than males in every age group.

Source: AIHW Table S29, 2023–24. Values converted from dollars to millions.
